This man knows no moderation. He learned desperate effort from bedbugs and harbors not even 1% anxiety about things not working out. He calls himself 'Bong-yi Jeong Seon-dal,' starts loan negotiations with a turtle ship drawn on a 500-won banknote, and convinces shipowners to buy ships with a shabby photo of an empty beach. He's the kind of person who breaks through conventional wisdom by asking, "Have you even tried it?" — finding new answers and making things happen. This is Chairman Chung Ju-yung.

In his book, he proudly shares his philosophy, forged through various events like the Soyanggang Dam, shipbuilding, expansion into the Middle East, and the Gyeongbu Expressway. The lessons from this man, full of ambition and drive—'pioneering spirit and conviction,' 'diligence,' 'trust,' 'frugality,' and 'right thinking'—are all invaluable. They make so much sense.

To a man who dedicates himself wholeheartedly to everything, 'moderation' must seem foolish, like letting precious time slip away in vain. For a man with the spirit to unleash a serious punch like One-Punch Man and obliterate everything, luck is unnecessary.

But then I wondered, perhaps the infinite potential and power of his unwavering will to achieve, which he so believes in, might be an unattainable level for others. Or perhaps what he considers 'moderate' might be someone else's maximum effort and best. Indeed, in his book, he mentioned that workers weren't working as hard as in the past, but rather, a little less hard. So, where does each person's desired level of achievement diverge?

I believe the biggest difference lies in Chairman Chung's childhood. He started working around the age of ten, commuting to the fields around 4 AM every day without fail. He toiled all day under the scorching sun, drenched in sweat, his bare hands covered in dirt, never straightening his back, and grew up to be a top farmer. By consistently enduring extreme experiences that no one could easily undertake today, the Chairman trained himself and, without realizing it, had already internalized strong self-conviction and will.

So, if I were to start farming now, like Chairman Chung, and train myself through extreme experiences, could I reach that level? Probably not. The essence of the Chairman's lesson isn't to do exactly as he did, but rather to consistently and diligently pursue something simple yet rigorous.

No one is born special. Everyone grows up ordinarily, goes to school, gets a job, or earns money, and lives their lives. While everyone lives a moderately ordinary life, if you're consistently moderate, anyone can become extraordinary. If you consistently repeat ordinary tasks—things anyone could do—to their extreme, in your own way, you'll transcend yourself at some point. Just as Chairman Chung farmed under the blazing sun, and Saitama for three years did 100 push-ups, 100 sit-ups, 100 squats, and a 10km run every day, without air conditioning or heating, and ate three meals a day.
