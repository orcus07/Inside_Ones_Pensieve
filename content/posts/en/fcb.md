In journalism, there's a term for slightly repackaging the substance of someone else's article — in Korean newsrooms, it's called *urakai* (우라까이), which roughly translates to "copying" or, in English, "He said/She said journalism." Interestingly, the word doesn't actually exist in Japanese. The closest equivalent is *uragaesu* (裏反す) — a verb meaning "to flip over" or "to change plans." It's likely that *uragaesu* evolved into the Korean *urakai* over time.

When a piece is written almost entirely in **[direct quotations](https://namu.wiki/w/%EC%9D%B8%EC%9A%A9%20%ED%91%9C%ED%98%84#%EC%A7%81%EC%A0%B1%20%EC%9D%B8%EC%9A%A9)**, it tends to look like this:

* 000 said, " ~ "
* Meanwhile, XX argued, " ~ "
* In response, @@ pushed back, " ~ "
* $$$ pointed out, " ~ "

Writing like this makes it hard to grasp the actual intent at a glance. Agenda-setting pieces pile on facts around a specific topic without revealing their angle. Single-chute questions only ask one side. Constant digressions scatter your focus. It starts to feel like someone is deliberately trying to distract you.

> **"Total Concentration Breathing"** **accelerates blood circulation and the heartbeat throughout the entire body.** Body temperature spikes — and **a human can become as strong as a demon.** You have to expand your lungs as much as possible. When you flood your blood with air and it's caught off guard, **your bones and muscles heat up and grow stronger in an instant.**

Total Concentration Breathing is a technique from the anime *Demon Slayer* — a method humans use to defeat demons far stronger than themselves. In the story, it makes you stronger, faster, and superhuman.

These days, there's just too much to read. And a lot of it feels strange — technically coherent but somehow off, seemingly sincere but weirdly hollow. In moments like these, the best thing you can do is take a deep breath, sharpen your focus, and work through complicated writing piece by piece. That's how you ask the real questions: Why were only these particular facts cited? Did I just agree with the argument out of laziness? Independent thinking requires that kind of effort. And writing like a human requires the constant practice of reading like one.
