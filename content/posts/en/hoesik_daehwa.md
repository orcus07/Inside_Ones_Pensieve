At a company dinner someone once said, “A lot of new people showed up. It’d be nice if we all got closer, but it doesn’t feel that way.”
When you hold a big get-together with that goal, the first chats with the people beside you feel vivid and clear. After that, the everyone-together conversation leaves a dull, clogged feeling — what was said grows fuzzy. Dense, meaningful talk becomes a group monologue.
You’d think talking to lots of people would create many relationships. Instead, it feels hollow. A short, focused conversation with one person is far more fulfilling. And that flow doesn’t appear right away. People pass each other, meet again and again, quietly getting comfortable. Then the dam of conversation slowly breaks.
When you see someone who’s close with another person, you want that closeness too. A desire that didn’t exist before bubbles up. It’d be nice if gathering alone made us instant friends, but getting close is proportional to the time people put into each other.
One hour with you is better than (n−1) hours with everyone.
