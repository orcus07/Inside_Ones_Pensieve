CES is in full swing, and I was genuinely surprised that Dell seems to be giving up on AI PCs. Kevin Terwilliger, their product lead, made it clear that this year's message isn't "AI-First." He publicly emphasized that consumers aren't buying PCs based on AI anymore. It feels like they're the first company to so boldly abandon an AI-First strategy.

In the age of AI, many manufacturers started cramming NPU components into their PCs and mobile devices. But it was an open secret that these expensive AI PCs and smartphones couldn't handle LLM inference or image generation. All they could really do was Zoom meeting background blurring, noise cancellation, and image correction—and even then, the manufacturers were footing the bill.

People don't want vague "AI"; they want the features that LLMs like ChatGPT or Gemini offer. Currently, you can't use those features without the cloud. If edge devices can't directly provide these capabilities, then AI PCs and smartphones are dead technology. In that case, it's better to ditch the AI marketing and just offer good, fast, and affordable products. It made me realize once again that companies that challenge component manufacturers are truly customer-centric. Their honesty felt refreshing, actually.

However, the future doesn't look bright for edge device manufacturers. Memory prices are skyrocketing. And Nvidia, the undisputed king of AI servers, is doing incredibly well, living up to its status as the top S&P 500 company. I heard their new Vera Rubin is about 10 times more token cost-effective than its predecessor, Grace-Blackwell.

As I keep observing this situation, I can't help but imagine a scenario where edge devices like PCs and smartphones become thinner (Thin Client), and all computing is replaced by subscription services. Consumers wouldn't need storage or CPU/NPU performance; they'd just buy a comfortable display and spend money on various subscription services. iPhones would be replaced by iCloud subscriptions, and PCs/mobile devices would become low-capacity, low-performance entry models, ultimately becoming dependent on AI services and cloud providers.

AWS's sudden 15% price hike for cloud services recently felt like a trigger for this future. While it's limited to high-performance EC2 instances, it was still a bit abrupt, considering that cloud instance prices usually drop over time after launch. ~~(Especially since they announced up to a 45% reduction for all instances just six months ago.)~~ If platform companies can raise prices and individuals are powerless, it feels less like a capitalist society where rational individuals act freely and more like a technological feudalism where platform dependency has grown.
