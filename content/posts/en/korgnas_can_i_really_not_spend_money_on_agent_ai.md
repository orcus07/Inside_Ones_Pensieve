# AI News Clipping from a Science-Averse Person

# Can I Really Resist Spending Money on Agent AI?

### My Candid Experience Spending a Fortune on Manus and Openclaw

I'm genuinely **feeling** that something big is happening these days. This isn't FOMO, the fear that everyone else is sprinting ahead without me, nor is it because of the industry-wide buzz about "Something Big is Happening" that's been circulating since last week. ~~(By the way, the post below talks about how the pace of AI replacing jobs has accelerated crazily since the release of GPT 5.3 Codex—it's a good read for understanding the current speed of change in the tech industry.)~~ The reason I'm feeling this shift is purely personal and simple: my spending on Agent AI has increased tenfold compared to my previous AI subscription fees. 💸

As I mentioned in the newsletter introduction, despite my engineering background, I'm a "science-averse person." So, you could define me as a non-technologist, and I'm pretty much anti-trendy in almost every area. I don't work in consumer marketing or promotions, so I don't immediately track micro-trends. Even when ChatGPT first came out, I tried it a few times, got bored, and stopped using it. ~~(Then as now, I prefer asking myself questions and answering them myself; it feels awkward talking to a complete stranger of an AI.)~~ I guess it's because I'm used to observing macro trends from the shadows of the semiconductor B2B industry. At the time, early AI features didn't seem very helpful for the semiconductor business, which deals with and requires uncommon information and context. I only subscribed to and used its deep research function when needed.

Then, one day, I tried using [Manus AI](https://news.hada.io/topic?id=19632) — a multi-AI agent service that Meta acquired for +$2B — to create a card news (a type of infographic). And just like that, 100,000 won vanished. 🤦‍♂️ The decisive reason I started spending money was that it delivered results immediately after I spoke. Even with vague instructions, it produced an output. Once I got a taste of the results, I started pursuing perfection, and from then on, I became possessed by prompt engineering, burning through credits like crazy. ~~(However, I eventually gave up in frustration when the tuned results went awry after some time and I tried to assign different tasks—maybe my prompts were just weird.)~~ That's when my perspective on AI changed a bit. "Oh, wow. It's actually pretty good?"

Then Openclaw came out, and out of sheer curiosity, I installed it on my old laptop. With a single, casually thrown natural language command, I saw it 1) organize folders and 2) immediately verify, implement, and execute a Vibe Coding project I'd done with an earlier AI. My eyes popped out again. 😲 Openclaw saves past contexts as .md files on the local PC and retrieves them for subsequent conversations, allowing for personalized, context-aware responses. Crucially, it's an "AI with long-term memory," which brings great satisfaction. After having it perform all the information retrieval, research, image analysis, and generation tasks I'd always wanted, hundreds of thousands of won had vanished again. 🤦‍♂️🤦‍♂️ Now, I've connected several models via API and manage tokens appropriately, checking them as often as I check my favorite newsletters or YouTube algorithm. ~~(Looking back, the token entry fee might have been a bit steep, but it was fun. For token optimization, let's make sure the Heartbeat function and context aren't read by cron or bots every time.)~~

So, that's my experience. If you ask me if I could go back to before I started using these, I'd probably say no. The moment Agent AI services make people feel, "Oh, it just does what I tell it to?" human behavior patterns change irreversibly. And if I'm starting to feel this change, everyone else will too. Perhaps Meta acquired Manus and OpenAI recruited Peter Steinberger, the creator of Openclaw, to preempt this tipping point. ~~(In fact, coders say they already felt such moments with GPT 5.3 Codex or Claude Opus 4.6, and Openclaw seems to be making many non-technologists feel those moments.)~~

Years from now, we might look back and say this year was the turning point from the SaaS-apocalypse (SaaS + Apocalypse) to the era of Agentic AI. It's a shift from an era where software was subscribed to on a per-seat model, to an era where API AI is billed based on usage/outcome. The cloud-based API call model will quickly gain traction due to its low initial barrier to entry and ease of operation. However, as service complexity grows, the billing proportion will increase too significantly. So, in fields like law or medicine (maintaining attorney-client or patient-doctor confidentiality) where independent, separate operation is still necessary, on-device fine-tuning models will likely operate in parallel.

However, as the tweet below suggests, algorithms can now be simply taken from anywhere, so the real bottleneck shifts to energy and compute. This applies to both API call and fine-tuning model scenarios. And among these, the core of inference compute lies in interconnection and memory bandwidth, meaning network companies and memory suppliers will likely remain consistently important. My work will probably increase significantly too. It's still enjoyable to watch one mainstream replaced and transformed by another.
