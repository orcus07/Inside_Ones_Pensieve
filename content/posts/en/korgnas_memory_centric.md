User experience solidifies into specific behavioral patterns as new paradigms emerge. Think of the PC's GUI or mobile's multi-touch — it's striking to realize both came from Apple, underscoring what a great company it is. When these behavioral patterns are implemented as an OS, users naturally become dependent on it, and the market expands. This is why companies that first captured these user behavioral patterns with an OS typically seized business leadership. Looking at the growth curves by IT paradigm below (written in '24), products like MSFT Windows and Apple's iPhone, which achieved this, became great and dominated the market for a long time.

![](/images/korgnas_korgnas_memory_centric_img1.jpg)

Meanwhile, the User Interface in the era of the AI paradigm began with LLM technology and took shape as chatbots that receive natural language input. We hoped for something different to emerge, but it seems to be converging into a chatbot form after all. In '24, when I was drafting the Flow Chart below, I thought Copilot or Apple Intelligence could serve as AI Agent Platforms. Both companies had clear strengths in Enterprise/Consumer Software, respectively, and it seemed it wouldn't be difficult for them to integrate those advantages well to create platforms that users would readily adopt. However, looking at the results, neither company managed to break free from the existing SaaS model's formula, allowing for the emergence of a new platform called Openclaw, developed by a solo developer outside the mainstream market. (It seems Clay Christensen's old theory of disruptive innovation, 'The Innovator's Dilemma,' is still valid.)

![](/images/korgnas_korgnas_memory_centric_img2.jpg)

If you look closely at Openclaw's workflow below, users still interact with the platform in natural language, solidifying that pattern, but an additional software layer is formed where the platform communicates with AI Agents or Sub-Agents. In essence, the user conveys context to the Agent platform, and the LLM platform then delegates tasks to specialized Sub-agents according to each context. From the user's perspective, they're not directly manipulating software or apps; they just speak in natural language, and all the work happens in the back-end. This shifts the point of contact for existing SaaS software and apps from the user to the Agent platform, i.e., Openclaw. The user only needs the result, and in the invisible back-end, Frontier Models compete to be selected via API based on performance, while on-device, different devices compete to be most suitable for orchestration. A new set of rules for the game is emerging, and naturally, SaaSpocalypse or the end of software becomes a rational scenario. (OpenAI, ironically, failed to convert the explosively growing ChatGPT user base in the early paradigm into a competitive advantage, seemingly stuck with free-tier users. Instead, it's caught in a chicken game with Anthropic, vying for Frontier Model competitiveness to lead the lucrative enterprise customer segment.)

![](/images/korgnas_korgnas_memory_centric_img3.jpg)

Considering who will dominate the AI era due to the spread of platforms like Openclaw, it seems to diverge slightly from the formula of previous IT paradigms. In the PC and mobile eras, the essence of competition was network effects. Securing users quickly was paramount, and companies were willing to incur initial losses for it. However, this paradigm seems to be shifting towards who can quickly preempt and control hardware/devices rather than users. As mentioned, in the Cloud API competition, it's a battle over who can provide tokens at the lowest cost, and in the on-device competition, it's about who can reasonably supply devices suitable for AI Agents.

From this perspective, Google and Apple seem to be a bit ahead. Google operates its API costs at the lowest due to TPU-based token cost optimization, its proprietary accelerator. Apple, with its self-designed chips, has devices suitable for AI Agents and, based on its supply chain negotiation power, is uniquely able to freeze PC/Smartphone prices. Surprisingly, the swing factor in all these scenarios is passively operating memory. If memory were cheap, the ecosystem would form around on-device scenarios, but a shortage is expected for the next few years, so it will likely remain a cloud-centric scenario for a while.

![](/images/korgnas_korgnas_memory_centric_img4.jpg)

Openclaw is now even appearing in Nvidia GTC 2026 PT, asserting its presence. Currently, Openclaw's only drawback is security, but Nvidia quickly partnered with Openclaw's founder to strengthen security, unveiling "Nemoclaw" — an enterprise open-source platform — at this GTC. Furthermore, they emphasized that their new server products, Vera-Rubin Ultra & LPU, are particularly effective for high-performance token throughput. From a monetization perspective, since the LLM OS automatically orchestrates user tasks and charges on a pay-as-you-go basis, any infrastructure solution that can effectively provide more expensive inference tokens via API to a broad customer base will translate into revenue. It's quite clever how they frame selling high-performance inference infrastructure at a premium to customers as directly leading to customer revenue. (Nvidia's own LLM, emotron, being bundled and emphasized as open-source is also clever.) Nvidia remains powerful even in the cloud scenario, which will be valid for the foreseeable future.
