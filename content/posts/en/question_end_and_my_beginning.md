At work, there are times when a colleague or team lead suddenly asks me a question.

For the person asking, it probably isn't a sudden question. They may be looking for something urgently, or they may have been searching through the problem in their own context before coming to me. By then, their head already holds what they have looked at, what they have tried, and where they got stuck.

But all I receive is the question sitting at the end of that process.

For them, the question comes after a long stretch of thought. For me, it is a problem I am encountering for the first time. They are at the end of the question. I am only just standing at the beginning.

This is when I often feel as though my mind isn't working. I have to understand the question and form an answer at the same time, yet I somehow feel that I should respond immediately. I also carry the assumption that my answer should already be refined.

Trying to give a clear, well-formed answer to a question I have just heard makes my mind stop. Sometimes I wonder whether this is simply the limit of my ability to think.

I think there are two ways to work through this.

The first is to reduce the problem until it is small enough for my thinking to handle. Instead of holding the question as one large mass, I break it into smaller pieces. I find its structure, divide it into steps, and think through them one by one.

I think step by step.

This means I do not have to solve a long problem all at once. I can begin with a smaller part that I can answer now. If the current length of my thought is short, I first divide the problem to fit that length. This is a matter of reasoning.

The other way is to extend the length of my thinking itself.

Sometimes my thoughts stop short. One thought ends before it can move into the next, or thoughts that could connect remain separate from one another. I need to practice joining those fragments and following them a little farther than I can now.

Learning how to handle a short thought is different from building the mental muscle to sustain a longer one. The first is reasoning for the problem in front of me. The second is closer to learning, and it takes time.

But at work, I cannot always secure enough time to think whenever someone suddenly asks me a question.

The other person is already looking for something urgently. Staying silent until I can give them a complete answer may be less useful than first answering within the limits of what I know. Even if the answer is not fully refined, it may relieve enough of their uncertainty to help them move to the next step.

So in that moment, I have little choice but to answer what I know. It is the best I can do then.

Still, an immediate answer can only contain as much thought as I can summon in that moment. When there is little time to think, the reasoning is short too. If I treat that as my complete answer, I become frustrated with myself. If I see it as my first answer to a question I have only just encountered, it looks a little different.

When I can, I will buy myself a little time. When I cannot, I will first answer what I can answer immediately. Then I will try to return to the thought that ended too soon and continue it later.

Their question arrives at the end, but that is where my thinking begins.
