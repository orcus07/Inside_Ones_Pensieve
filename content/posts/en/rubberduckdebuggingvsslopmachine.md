Rubber duck debugging is a coding tip introduced in 'The Pragmatic Programmer.' It's simply about placing a rubber duck next to you, as shown in the picture, and explaining how your code works, line by line. This seemingly simple act of explaining and reading your code can lead to sudden insights. Laura Huang's book, 'Edge: Turning Adversity into Advantage,' explains this process in more detail. She says that intuition is a fleeting moment of insight that emerges when accumulated prior knowledge interacts with a present stimulus, resulting in a "Eureka!" moment.

The important thing is that when I explain a problem to myself, the answer often just appears. It won't happen every time, but that's okay. Even if you don't find the exact answer you're looking for right away, those experiences and stimuli gradually build up your prior knowledge. These treasures you discover through self-dialogue and reflection shape and define who you are now and who you'll become.

However, if you look closely, the process of finding the right answer and refining intuition resembles the process of training AI and improving its performance. The reason current language models have grown into LLMs is that when models are trained with a lot of data, characteristics unintended by developers emerge on their own. AI capabilities have advanced so much that they're now incredibly powerful. Due to FOMO—the fear of being left behind if you don't use it—or simply curiosity, AI seems to have become an integral part of our daily lives.

But I also worry that this powerful tool might take away our abilities. If you think about it, calculators degraded our mental math skills, and smartphones diminished our memory. If we become increasingly dependent on AI, the quality of our language and communication—how we interpret the world and express ourselves—will be the first to suffer. Then, the number of black boxes in our minds, where understanding is deferred, will increase. That's because we haven't engaged our hands and minds to think and reason directly. As AI configures and automates parts of our thinking like an API, our identity is threatened. If we keep pulling the AI conversation lever like a "slot" machine, we'll only generate AI "slop."

So, these days, I'm trying to use senses I haven't used much. I try to do mental math. I try to remember the phone numbers of loved ones. For knowledge and learning, instead of just pressing an AI button or flicking my fingers, I try to expand my knowledge by reading books or by writing things down. It seems like the era has come where even a generalist like me—who didn't adapt well to science in my youth, couldn't become a T-shaped individual, and lingered on the fringes—can now dream of being a Renaissance person or a polymath. That is, if I can spin the flywheel of "directly" thinking and "directly" executing anything. ~~Not a "slop" machine.~~
