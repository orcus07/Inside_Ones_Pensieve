I made a morning routine for thought experiments and sorting my ideas.

When my head is clearest, I spread out a sheet of paper and jot down whatever comes to mind. Sometimes it all falls neatly into place; other times arrows multiply everywhere. Today was the latter.

On the lower part of the page a question was written at a slant.

"Did I secretly want my work to be praised?"

It was after I felt bothered by feedback I’d received at work.

Hearing the words made me feel bad. My first reaction was to doubt myself. Wasn't what I wanted not feedback but praise? Was I uncomfortable because the reply differed from what I'd hoped to hear?

But that alone didn't explain why the way it was said kept lingering.

So I wrote this at the top of the page.

"The essence of subjecting my expression to scrutiny before others."

And beneath it I drew two inequalities.

> `Truth > People`

It's the way of correcting by saying, "That's wrong," when you find a mistake.

> `Truth < People`

It's the way of asking, "Could it be phrased like this?" about the same words.

I'm more comfortable with the second. Still, I don't think the first is wrong. Hurtful words and wrong words are different. Even if it's a bit uncomfortable, if it can bring me closer to the truth, I need the chance to hear it.

Both approaches give people the opportunity to speak without hesitation.

The problem is what comes next.

If bluntness keeps repeating from only one side, some people will speak without restraint while others get busy interpreting what was said — figuring out the meaning, re-explaining their intent, even sorting out misunderstandings that shouldn't have happened.

One person's honesty becomes someone else's job.

I wrote that down as the cost of communication.

I mentally placed the person who gave me feedback somewhere between an editor and a critic. This wasn't about their job title; it was a metaphor to help me think whether they were trying to help revise the work or merely stopping at pointing out faults.

Of course, that boundary isn't always clear. I might have been the one who took comments about the work as an evaluation of my ability or intentions as a whole. In that case, I want to guard myself before accepting the feedback.

Conversely, if remarks about the work had quietly started to judge a person's character and intentions, there's no need to accept that as feedback for the piece.

Maybe it was both.

I wanted my work to be praised, and that feedback also carried an unnecessary cost of communication. Just because one is true doesn't make the other disappear.

I can acknowledge and learn that there are many things to fix in my work.

Separately, I can reconsider whether that feedback was good feedback.
